## Problem Statement

Cancer trials struggle to enroll: in one study of 4,004 breast, lung and colorectal trials, only 49% reached their enrollment goal, and the rate fell to 36% for trials started in 2017-2019. Meanwhile, cancer patients outside major centers, starting with Latin America, rarely hear about trials they could qualify for, rarely get a specialist second opinion, and don't know what enrolling would cost or require (travel, insurance, visas, follow-up). Paraguay hosts very few cancer trials, so patients there have to look abroad.

## What Makes This Cool

- One engine reads a patient's records in **any language** and matches them against trials **anywhere in the world**, then explains the result in the patient's own language.
- It doesn't stop at a list of trials. It answers the real question families have: "Can we actually do this, and what would it take?"
- Remote tumor boards connect a local oncologist with a Spanish-speaking specialist abroad, often a Latin American doctor at a top cancer center, for a 30-minute review of a hard case.

## Constraints

- Founder is a full-time undergraduate (Penn, Biophysics & Economics) working in a cancer immunotherapy lab, so hours are limited.
- Medical, legal and privacy risk: patient data crosses borders and specialists advise across licensing boundaries.
- A strong, funded competitor (Massive Bio) already does AI oncology trial matching, including in 9 Latin American countries through a Spanish-language partner, and has an OpenAI partnership.

## Premises

1. Match patients to trials anywhere in the world, in any language. Latin America is where we start, not a limit.
2. Matching alone is becoming a commodity, so it can't be the whole edge.
3. The hard, valuable part is turning a match into an enrollment: costs, insurance and standard-of-care billing, travel coverage, visas, language, and follow-up back home.
4. Tumor boards give doctors a reason to bring cases (and records) to us, which is what matching companies struggle to get.
5. Two separate products share one engine: the open trial matcher is not limited to tumor-board patients.

Ruled out during the session: bringing trial sites to Paraguay (founder judged it unrealistic, since sponsors prefer established sites).

## Approaches Considered

### Approach A: Navigator by hand + small tool
Help 5-10 real patients end to end over WhatsApp, with an AI case-prep and matching tool built in this repo. Fastest learning about what actually blocks enrollment. Doesn't scale by itself.

### Approach B: Full platform first
Global, any-language matching + feasibility checks + human navigation + tumor boards, as a product. The end state, but built before talking to a single patient or doctor.

### Approach C: Free tool for doctors
Case summary + global trial matches for oncologists, who refer patients. Lower legal risk than tumor boards, but slow doctor adoption.

## Recommended Approach

**One shared engine, two front doors, started by hand.**

- **Engine (build first, in this repo):** paste or upload a patient summary in any language → structured case (diagnosis, stage, biomarkers, prior treatments, location) → matching recruiting trials from ClinicalTrials.gov → plain-language explanation in the patient's language → a feasibility checklist per trial (distance, travel support, insurance and cost questions to ask the site).
- **Front door 1, Trial matcher:** open to patients, families and any doctor. Differentiates from Massive Bio on languages, plain explanations and enrollment help.
- **Front door 2, Tumor boards:** doctor-to-doctor case review. The local doctor stays responsible for treatment; specialists give written second opinions only; patients consent and cases are de-identified. Every case also runs through the engine.
- **Business model:** trial sponsors and CROs pay per patient who enrolls. Tumor boards are free or cheap at first and drive patients to the matcher.

Why: the engine is the shared core both products need. Running 5 real cases by hand shows which part (matching, costs, travel or follow-up) actually decides whether a patient enrolls, before we build the full platform.

## Open Questions

- Will Paraguayan oncologists share de-identified cases with a student-founded startup? Who is the first champion?
- Will diaspora specialists volunteer at first, and what do we pay them later?
- How do sponsors actually pay recruitment partners (per referral, per screened, per randomized patient), and what proof do they need?
- Legal: doctor-to-doctor consult rules across borders, data protection in Paraguay and the US (HIPAA), and whether the AI could be classed as a medical device. Get a lawyer through Penn's resources before any real patient data is used.
- Can we partner with Massive Bio rather than compete (e.g., as the enrollment and logistics layer for Spanish-speaking patients)?

## Success Criteria

- 5 real cases reviewed by a specialist through a hand-run tumor board.
- The engine produces a correct case summary and at least one relevant recruiting trial for each case, confirmed by a doctor.
- At least 1 patient takes a concrete enrollment step (contacts a trial site or gets screened).

## Distribution Plan

Starts as a web app (the engine) used by the founder during hand-run cases, then opened to doctors and patients. Deployment pipeline to be decided in /plan-eng-review.

## Next Steps

1. **Build the engine prototype in this repo:** text in any language → structured case → ClinicalTrials.gov search (public API) → plain-language summary + feasibility checklist. Test only on synthetic or published case reports, never real patient data, until consent and legal review are in place.
2. **Line up doctors:** 1 oncologist in Paraguay who will send 5 hard cases with patient consent, names removed; 1-2 Spanish-speaking specialists (Penn network, co-authors) who will review on a 30-minute call.
3. **Run the 5 cases by hand,** using the engine for prep, and write down what stopped or helped enrollment for each patient.
4. **Talk to 3 people who buy recruitment:** a CRO or sponsor recruitment manager, to learn how they pay and what proof they need.
